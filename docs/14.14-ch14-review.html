14.14 Chapter Review

14.14.1 Chapter summary

Strong final communication makes a traceable argument from problem to evidence, interpretation, recommendation, implementation, and evaluation. Reports and presentations should be tailored to their audiences, technically accurate, visually coherent, explicit about limitations, and accompanied by the files and documentation needed for responsible handoff.

14.14.2 Common mistakes

  • organizing the report around the order in which analysis was performed;
  • writing an executive summary that describes sections but omits conclusions and actions;
  • making recommendations that cannot be traced to specific evidence;
  • placing dense report text on presentation slides; and
  • delivering outputs without reproducible files, definitions, assumptions, and use limitations.

14.14.3 Final communication checklist

14.14.4 Exercises

Exercise 1. Revise this recommendation: “NVRW should use social media to attract more young customers and increase revenue by 15 percent.”

One Possible Answer

NVRW should test a targeted digital campaign for the eligible audience and service category identified by the acquisition and retention analysis. The pilot should use a defined budget and comparison period, with registrations, acquisition cost, twelve-month retention, and contribution margin as evaluation measures. This revision removes the unsupported demographic stereotype and unproven 15 percent effect.

Exercise 2. Build a recommendation-evidence matrix for the three recommendations in the NVRW synthesis appendix. Add one counterargument, one implementation risk, and one evaluation measure for each recommendation.

Check Your Work

Every recommendation row should cite specific findings, state the inference connecting the evidence to action, acknowledge a reasonable counterargument, identify an implementation risk and mitigation, and name a measurable outcome with timing and ownership. A recommendation without traceable evidence or a feasible evaluation measure is not ready for delivery.

Exercise 3. Draft an executive summary no longer than 250 words. It must state the decision, three findings, three actions, and the most important limitation without relying on technical terminology.

Check Your Work

An excellent summary is self-contained and leads with the decision. It states the three findings in plain language, connects each proposed action to those findings, and explains the limitation that most affects use of the results. It does not list report sections, introduce unsupported evidence, or require the reader to consult a technical appendix to understand the conclusion.

Exercise 4. Create the eight-slide presentation described in the synthesis appendix. Every slide title must be a supported message rather than a topic label.

One Possible Answer

A possible sequence is: (1) the decision NVRW must make; (2) validated evidence supports focused action; (3) demand differs across facilities; (4) low early engagement identifies retention risk; (5) capacity pressure is concentrated; (6) projections depend on population and utilization assumptions; (7) three measured actions address the evidence; and (8) the next step is an evaluated pilot and monitoring plan.

14.14.5 Questions for discussion

  1. When should a technically important limitation appear in the executive summary?
  2. Can a recommendation be useful when its expected financial effect cannot be estimated?
  3. What information belongs in a handoff but not in the report?
